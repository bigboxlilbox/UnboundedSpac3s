Five documents every new beauty business needs before the first client
description: From consultation forms to cancellation policies, here are the five documents that protect a new beauty or hair business, keep clients clear on what to expect, and make you look established from day one.

You can be brilliant at lashes, brows, nails or hair and still find the business side of things a mess. That's not a failing. Nobody trains you in paperwork at beauty school.
But the businesses that grow steadily, the ones with full books and clients who rebook without being chased, almost always have a few basics in place early. Not complicated systems. Just clear documents that set expectations before anyone sits in your chair.
Here are the five worth having before your first client.
## 1. A client consultation form
This is the one that protects you most, and the one most new beauty businesses skip.
Before any treatment, you need a record of your client's medical history, allergies, medications, previous reactions, and anything that might affect the treatment or the result. For some treatments it's a legal and insurance requirement. For all of them, it's a record that you asked.
Your form should cover:
- Contact details and date of birth
- Relevant medical conditions and medications
- Known allergies and previous reactions
- Any recent treatments or procedures
- A space for the client to confirm the information is accurate and sign
Keep these securely and follow UK GDPR, since this is health information and it's treated more strictly than a name and email. Store them somewhere locked or password protected, and don't keep them longer than you need to.
## 2. A patch test record
If you offer tinting, lash extensions, colour services or anything else requiring a patch test, you need a written record showing the test was offered, when it was done, and the result.
Insurers ask for this. So do clients, occasionally, when something goes wrong. A verbal "yes I did that" is worth very little after the fact.
Include the date, the product used, the site of the test, the result, and the client's signature. If a client declines a patch test, record that too, along with your decision about whether to proceed.
## 3. A treatment consent form
Different from the consultation form, though they often sit together. This is where the client confirms they understand what the treatment involves, the expected result, possible side effects, and the aftercare required.
It matters most for treatments with a real recovery or risk element, but it's good practice across the board. It shifts the conversation from "I thought it would look different" to a shared understanding you both agreed to in advance.
## 4. A clear booking and cancellation policy
This is the document that will save you the most money.
Late cancellations and no-shows are the quiet killer of a new beauty business. An empty two hour slot on a Saturday isn't just lost income, it's a slot someone else wanted.
Your policy should say:
- How much notice you need to cancel or reschedule, usually twenty four or forty eight hours
- What happens if that notice isn't given, whether that's a fee, a lost deposit, or a strike system
- Whether you take deposits, how much, and whether they're refundable
- How late a client can arrive before the appointment is treated as missed
Then, and this is the important bit, send it to every client when they book. A policy nobody has read isn't a policy.
Be realistic about what you'll enforce. If you know you'll never charge a regular for a late cancellation, don't write a rule that says you will. Write one you'll actually stand behind.
## 5. An aftercare sheet
The cheapest way to protect your reputation.
Most complaints about beauty treatments come down to what happened after the client left, not what you did while they were there. Lashes get wet too soon. Colour gets washed the same evening. Nails get picked at.
A simple aftercare sheet, handed over or sent by message after every appointment, does two things: it gives the client the best chance of a good result, and it gives you something to point to if they come back unhappy about something the aftercare covered.
Keep it short and specific to the treatment. What to avoid, for how long, what's normal, and when to get in touch.
## Putting it together
None of these need to be elaborate. A single page each is often enough. What matters is that they exist, that clients see them, and that you use them consistently rather than only when you remember.
If you're starting from nothing, the quickest route is to take properly structured templates and fill in your own treatments, timings and policies. That gets you set up in an afternoon rather than putting it off for another six months.
Your work is the thing clients pay for. The paperwork is what makes sure they keep coming back, and that you're covered when something doesn't go to plan.
