Most creative work doesn't go wrong at the end. It goes wrong at the beginning, quietly, in the gap between what the client imagined and what you thought they asked for.
Then it shows up later as round seven of revisions, an invoice nobody's paying, or your logo appearing on merchandise you never licensed.
Two short documents prevent almost all of it: a brief that captures what's being made, and a set of terms that covers how you'll work and what happens when things change. Neither needs to be long. Both need to exist before you start.
## Why the brief is the most valuable document you'll write
A brief is not admin. It's the moment you and the client agree on the same picture.
Written properly, it protects you in a way contracts can't, because it defines the thing itself. When a client says "this isn't what I wanted," a good brief turns a vague disagreement into a specific conversation about something you both signed off.
What belongs in it:
**The objective.** What this work needs to achieve, in the client's words. Not "a new logo" but "a logo that works on packaging and on a small phone screen, aimed at parents of young children."
**The audience.** Who it's for. This single line prevents an enormous amount of subjective feedback, because it moves the question from "do I like it" to "does this work for them."
**Deliverables, listed precisely.** Not "brand design" but exactly what lands: how many concepts, which file formats, which sizes, how many pages, how long the video runs. Specificity here is what makes scope creep visible.
**What's out of scope.** Worth stating plainly. Social media templates, printed materials, ongoing edits, and copywriting are all things clients often assume are included.
**Timeline, including their bits.** Your delivery dates and their feedback deadlines. Most creative projects slip because the client took three weeks to respond, not because the designer was slow.
**References and constraints.** Brand colours, existing guidelines, things they've seen and liked, things to avoid.
Send it back to the client and get a yes in writing before you start. That yes is the thing you'll be glad of in week five.
## The terms that save creatives the most grief
Your terms sit alongside the brief and cover how the working relationship runs.
**Revisions.** State how many rounds are included and what happens after that, usually an hourly or per-round rate. Two or three rounds is standard. Without this, revisions are infinite by default, and the client isn't being unreasonable, they just don't know where the line is.
Define what a round means too: one consolidated set of feedback, not five separate emails over a fortnight.
**Payment terms.** A deposit before work starts, usually between thirty and fifty per cent. Payment terms for the balance, commonly fourteen days. What happens if payment is late, whether that's interest or paused work.
If you're a sole trader or small business in the UK, you're entitled to charge statutory interest on late commercial payments. Even if you rarely enforce it, having it written down changes how people treat your invoices.
**When the copyright transfers.** This is the big one, and the one most creatives get wrong.
Under UK law, you own the copyright in what you create unless you agree otherwise in writing. That's your default position, and it's a good one. Make it explicit that ownership passes to the client on full payment, not before. It means unpaid work stays yours, which is a far stronger position than chasing an invoice with nothing to hold.
**The licence, if you're not transferring ownership.** If you're keeping the copyright and licensing the work instead, say exactly what they can do with it: which media, which territories, for how long, and whether they can modify it. Illustrators and photographers in particular should think carefully before handing over full ownership as standard.
**Moral rights and credit.** Whether you'll be credited, and where. Worth asking for.
**Portfolio use.** Your right to show the work in your portfolio and on social media, and whether there's a delay before you can, which matters for unreleased products.
**Cancellation.** What happens if the client pulls out halfway. Typically you keep the deposit and invoice for work completed to that point.
**Third party costs.** Fonts, stock images, printing and hosting. Say who pays, and get approval before spending.
## Keeping it light
None of this needs to feel heavy or adversarial. The best framing is simply: "Here's the brief so we're building the same thing, and here's how I work, so there are no surprises on either side."
Clients rarely object. The ones who do are usually telling you something useful.
## Where to start
You don't need to draft these from nothing. A solid brief template and a set of creative terms you adapt to each project takes an afternoon to set up and then serves every client after that.
Get them in place while things are calm, not in the middle of a project that's already gone sideways. That's when they're hardest to introduce and when you need them most.
